\section{Threat Model and Measurement Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

\subsection{Adversary}

The attacker controls the message text and nothing else: no access to weights,
gradients, logits or the detector's code, and no ability to retrain it. The
attacker additionally wants the message to remain readable, so the edit budget
is bounded: at most 15\% of the message's units (characters for the
character-level attack, whitespace tokens for the word-level attack) may
change, and the change is measured, not estimated.

\paragraph{Two access levels, reported separately.} Not every attack in this
paper assumes the same interface, and the paper never pools them.
\emph{Verdict access} returns the one-word generated answer: malicious or
benign, one bit per query. That is what a spammer probing a filter receives,
and it is the oracle for the character and random-word attacks
(\S\ref{sec:results}). \emph{Probability access} additionally exposes
$P(\text{malicious})$ --- here by scoring the two permitted answer tokens ---
and only the saliency-guided attack uses it (\S\ref{sec:saliency}), because a
ranking needs a number and one bit per candidate cannot produce one. Every
row in every table records which channel produced it, the two channels are
never mixed in a single comparison, and the disagreement between them is itself
reported (\S\ref{sec:discussion}). The ablation of \S\ref{sec:ablation} holds
the oracle fixed across arms precisely so that the cost of dropping from
probability access to verdict access is measured rather than assumed.

The verdict-only adversary is a deliberately weak one, and its ASR is a lower
bound for that reason as well. A white-box attacker with logit access, an
embedding-space attack, or a saliency-guided word search would do better. The
point of a weak adversary is that a flip under it is unambiguously bad news
for the detector: nothing exotic happened, the message simply stopped looking
suspicious.

\subsection{The four rules that make an ASR quotable}

\paragraph{Rule 1 --- conditional denominator, re-queried.}
Only messages the clean model classified \emph{malicious} are attacked. The
original text is queried again inside the attack run instead of trusting the
baseline label, and a sample whose original is no longer read as malicious is
\emph{excluded} from the denominator rather than counted as a failed attack. In
our runs this happened 52 times, and each exclusion is recorded with its reason.
The 52 split into exactly two kinds, and the split is not the one an earlier
draft reported. Forty-three are samples the saliency attack's probability route
read as benign on the original --- all of them on Qwen2.5-3B, which is a channel
artefact discussed in \S\ref{sec:discussion}. The other nine are word-attack
samples whose perturbation came out identical to the original, so no edit was
measured and there was nothing to score. No original was dropped for any other
reason: the generator route never re-read a baseline-caught message as benign,
so the character and saliency runs on the other two models exclude nothing at
all.

\paragraph{Rule 2 --- the same prompt on both sides.}
The baseline and the attack call the identical message template, including the
sample's own task description, so an e-mail is never scored with the SMS
prompt. A baseline-versus-attack comparison across different prompts measures
the prompt.

\paragraph{Rule 3 --- one shared budget, exact distance.}
The 15\% budget is a single allowance for the whole perturbation, not one per
operation, and the perturbation rate is the exact Levenshtein distance divided
by the length of the original. Both details are load-bearing. Giving each
operation its own allowance let the real rate reach 22\% while the paper called
it 15\%; and estimating the distance from a `difflib' opcode tally
reported 204 edits for 56 deleted characters on a repetitive message, because
`difflib' does not compute a minimal edit script.

\paragraph{Rule 4 --- name the similarity you actually computed.}
We report word-set Jaccard overlap and call it Jaccard. No semantic-similarity
model is used anywhere in this study, so no BERTScore is claimed. For the
character attack we additionally note that Jaccard is the wrong instrument: a
homoglyph substitution changes the token, so word-set overlap collapses even
though a human reads the same message.

\subsection{Perturbations}

\textbf{Character level.} Four operations share the budget: homoglyph
substitution (Latin `a', `e', `o', `p', `c', `x', `y', `i', `s' replaced by
Cyrillic codepoints), random deletion, random insertion of lowercase letters,
and adjacent-position swap (costing two edits).

\textbf{Word level.} Four operations share the budget: replacement of a word by
one of its listed synonyms from a hand-written 14-entry table, word deletion,
word duplication, and word swap. The synonym table is deliberately small, which
weakens the attack and strengthens the negative result.

\textbf{How many attempts, and what that costs.} The two random attacks give
each sample up to three independent attempts with a per-sample seeded random
generator, stopping at the first flip, so a message can cost one to four
queries: one for the re-queried original plus one per attempt. On the recorded
runs that comes to 3.9 queries per message and 8{,}047 queries for the 2{,}079
attacked messages --- the number of messages and the number of queries are
different quantities and are reported separately throughout. The saliency attack
is not attempt-based: it probes every candidate position once and then re-queries
after each accepted edit, 22.9 queries per message on average. The earlier draft
of this paragraph said ``one attempt per sample'', which the per-sample records
contradict; the attempt column in every row is the correction.

Every attempt's perturbed text,
verdict, edit count and rate is written to the per-sample CSV, so the tables can
be rebuilt without the model.

\subsection{Statistics}

An ASR of $k$ flips out of $n$ attacks is a binomial proportion. We report the
95\% Wilson score interval, which stays inside $[0,1]$ and, crucially, does not
collapse at the boundary: a row with $k=0$ of $n=188$ has upper bound 2.0\%, not
0\%. A percentile bootstrap over the same rows (10{,}000 resamples, fixed seed)
is computed as a cross-check and agrees with Wilson wherever $k>0$; where
$k=0$ the bootstrap degenerates to $[0,0]$ and we quote Wilson instead. The
interval covers sampling variability over \emph{messages}, not variation of the
perturbation seed; estimating the latter requires a second run over the same
samples, which is what the seed sweep below does.

\paragraph{Seed variation.} The intervals above cover variation over messages,
not over the perturbation generator. The runner therefore takes \texttt{--seed}
and a sweep driver (\texttt{src/run\_seed\_sweep.py}) re-runs one arm at several
seeds, so the seed-to-seed spread can be reported next to the sampling interval
rather than absorbed into it. The sweep is implemented; its cells are not part
of the numbers in this version, and \S\ref{sec:discussion} lists that as a gap
rather than implying the intervals cover it.
