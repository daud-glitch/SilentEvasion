\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Language models for email and SMS triage.}
Using pre-trained language models without fine-tuning (zero-shot classification)
is now a standard baseline for security triage, that is, for sorting incoming
messages into harmful and safe. Rojas-Galeano tested models on SpamAssassin
without extra training: an F1 score of 90\% for Flan-T5 on short emails and 95\%
for GPT-4 on summarised emails~\cite{rojas2024zeroshot}. Eilertsen et al.\ tried
zero-shot and few-shot prompts for phishing detection. Accuracy varied between
models, and they asked whether the answers come from what the model already
knows or from the examples in the prompt~\cite{eilertsen2025intent}. Koide et
al.\ built a model from email headers and bodies and reported 99.70\%
accuracy with GPT-4~\cite{koide2024chatspamdetector}. Wang et al.\ added external web context to SMS triage inside an agent pipeline
and reached 98.8\% overall
accuracy~\cite{wang2025smishx}. Thapa et al.\ studied small language models and
found that rewritten emails lower detection performance~\cite{thapa2025quantized}.
Our study is about measurement rules rather than model design. We keep the
model and the prompt fixed and apply careful counting rules to see how text
edits change the final decision.

\paragraph{Attacking security text filters.}
Shafee et al.\ generated believable text to attack threat-intelligence
pipelines. Their text misled the models, producing a 75\% false-positive
rate when ChatGPT-4o classified the messages~\cite{shafee2026falsealarms}.
Kulkarni et al.\ generated fake phishing pages: small models failed, while
vision-language models held up better~\cite{kulkarni2024mltollm}. Our setting is
the raw message text, not a web page, and our attacker makes small edits instead
of writing new text from scratch. That matches what a real attacker needs,
because the victim still has to read the message.

\paragraph{Small text edits (perturbations) and their budgets.}
Character-level attacks change single letters. DeepWordBug added typos and
letter swaps~\cite{gao2018deepwordbug}, and HotFlip formalized gradient-based
character flips~\cite{ebrahimi2018hotflip}. Word-level attacks replace whole
words while keeping the meaning. TextFooler searches for synonyms
greedily~\cite{jin2020textfooler}, and PWWS ranks words by how much a replacement
changes the model's confidence (its saliency)~\cite{ren2019pwws}. The TextAttack
library puts these methods into standard goals, limits and
transformations~\cite{morris2020textattack}. We test two search strengths. The
first picks a synonym at random from a small table; it stands for a simple
attacker and gives a lower bound on evasion. The second ranks word swaps by their
measured effect on the model (Section~\ref{sec:saliency}), and we call it the guided
attack. In Section~\ref{sec:ablation} we also run TextFooler's leave-one-out word
ranking as a reference arm. We use PWWS and TextFooler to define the search
logic, not to compare our numbers with the ones they report.

\paragraph{Cross-model transfer, and how such results should be evaluated.}
Yuan et al.\ showed that adversarial text often transfers across different
neural models, and built ensembles that attack several models at
once~\cite{yuan2021transfer}. He et al.\ extracted model parameters to build
transferable attacks against a target system~\cite{he2021extraction}. These
studies motivate our cross-model transfer matrix in Section~\ref{sec:transfer}. In our
tests, edits found on one model rarely work on another, because our attacker runs
a bounded local search rather than learning directions that carry across models.
On statistics, Carlini et al.\ showed that many robustness evaluations contain
flaws and proposed standard practice for threat models~\cite{carlini2019evaluating}.
Zimmermann et al.\ used active tests to find weaknesses in 11 of 13 published
defences~\cite{zimmermann2022confidence}. Recent work also stresses the need for
exact confidence intervals in robustness certificates~\cite{voracek2024statistical}.
We follow that practice: every evasion rate in this paper reports its numerator,
its total count and an exact Wilson interval~\cite{nistwilson}.
