\begin{abstract}
Security teams often use language models (AI text classifiers) to sort incoming
emails and text messages. The model decides whether a message is \emph{malicious}
(harmful or dangerous) or \emph{benign} (safe and normal) before a human analyst
reviews it. In this paper, we study what happens when an attacker makes small,
careful text edits (known as perturbations) to bypass the classifier. We show
that evasion rates can only be measured accurately under a \emph{conditional}
testing rule: researchers must only attack messages that the model originally
caught, verify each original message again during testing, and limit the attacker
to a realistic edit budget per sample.

We test this method on three open, lightweight language models (1.7 billion to
3.8 billion parameters) without extra fine-tuning (zero-shot) on phishing emails
and SMS spam (smishing). We test 2{,}079 caught messages using 8{,}047 model
queries under a 15\% total edit budget. At first glance, the models appear
quite resistant to simple edits. Small character changes (like typos or
look-alike letters) flip only 0.0--5.0\% of caught messages. Simple word swaps
flip only 1.0--5.1\% while modifying about half as many letters.

However, a more targeted search changes the picture. When we use a saliency-guided
word attack (ranking word replacements by their actual impact on the model's
decision), the attack success rate rises to 4.6\%, compared to 2.7\% for random
word swaps. This guided attack achieves 46 flips across 1{,}001 attacks, averaging
23 model queries per message. In direct paired comparisons on the exact same
messages, the saliency attack flips 33 messages that random edits miss, while
random edits flip only 7 that saliency misses (exact McNemar $p=4\times10^{-5}$).
A similar but smaller gap appears between word and character edits (26 versus 10,
$p=0.011$). This demonstrates that model vulnerability is concentrated in a few
key words rather than spread evenly across the vocabulary. Evasion rates
depend heavily on the quality of the attacker's search strategy.

Two other key findings help interpret these numbers. First, text edits do not
transfer well across different models. When we test saved edits against other
models, only 0.9\% of caught messages flip off the diagonal. Second, continuous
probability scores (the model's internal confidence) do not always match the
final generated text decision. The two readouts agree on 941 of 1{,}001 rows,
matching perfectly for one model but diverging on another. We report final
text decisions throughout and treat this gap as an important measurement
finding. For defenses, cleaning look-alike characters stops all character-level
evasions, but has little effect on word-level swaps (7.5\% to 6.9\%). Adjusting
decision cutoffs (re-thresholding) to maintain a 5\% false-alarm budget can
actually increase evasions for models with uncalibrated probability scores.

Our main practical takeaway is that with small edit budgets, the primary
limitation of small triage models is basic classification accuracy rather than
susceptibility to random text edits. Furthermore, attack success rates must
always be reported with their exact denominator (sample count), a statistical
confidence interval, and the specific search method used. A result of 0 out of
188 is an upper limit of 2.0\%, not proof of absolute security. We release all
frozen datasets, attack code, and individual sample records to support
reproducible security evaluations.
\end{abstract}
