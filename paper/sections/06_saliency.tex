\section{Where is the model fragile? A saliency-guided word attack}
\label{sec:saliency}

The word attack of \S\ref{sec:results} picks its replacement at random from a
small synonym table. It answers ``does \emph{any} word-level edit break the
model'' and leaves the location of the weakness unmeasured: a flip caused by
deleting the word \emph{urgent} and a flip caused by replacing the word
\emph{the} are the same event in that accounting. This section asks the sharper
question, and pays for the answer in queries.

\paragraph{Method.} We use the ordering of PWWS~\cite{ren2019pwws}. For a
message $x$ that the model reads as malicious with probability $p_0$, a content
word $i$ and a candidate replacement $w$, let $p_{i,w} = P(\text{malicious} \mid
x \text{ with word } i \text{ replaced by } w)$ be measured with the same prompt.
The importance of a word (its saliency) and the priority of a substitution are
\begin{equation}
S_i = p_0 - p_{i,w}, \qquad
\phi_{i,w} = \alpha \, \tilde{S}_i + (1-\alpha)\, p_{i,w},
\end{equation}
where $\tilde{S}_i$ is $S_i$ divided by the largest saliency in the message and
$\alpha = 0.5$. Substitutions are applied greedily in descending $\phi$; after
each accepted edit the verdict is queried again, and the attack stops at the
first flip or when the shared 15\% word budget is used up.

The normalization of $S$ is not only for appearance. With raw saliency and
$\alpha = 0.5$, $\phi_{i,w} = \tfrac{1}{2}(p_0 - p_{i,w}) + \tfrac{1}{2} p_{i,w}
= \tfrac{1}{2} p_0$ for \emph{every} candidate: the ranking flattens to a
constant, and the greedy loop then applies substitutions in whatever order the
words happen to appear in the message. Because $p_0$ is common to all candidates,
only the scale of $S$ matters, and the scale is exactly what the normalization
fixes. Our first version of this module had that defect; the unit test that now
guards it (\texttt{tests/check\_saliency.py}) checks that a high-saliency word
outranks a neutral one at $\alpha = 0.5$.

\paragraph{Candidate replacements.} Two sources, recorded for every substitution:
a curated security word list (66 entries: \emph{verify} $\rightarrow$
\emph{confirm/validate}, \emph{prize} $\rightarrow$ \emph{award/reward}, \ldots)
and WordNet~\cite{miller1995wordnet} synonyms ordered by how often the word
occurs. The ordering matters more than it looks: WordNet's third sense of a word
is where synonyms stop being paraphrases (\emph{tone} $\rightarrow$
\emph{timber}), and such a substitution would be counted as meaning-preserving
when it is not. Multi-word replacements are excluded so that the 15\% budget
counts words and nothing else. Candidates are capped at two per position and
twelve positions per message, which is what keeps the query cost bounded.

\paragraph{Two answer channels.} This attack needs a number, not a paragraph: at
every candidate it asks the endpoint for $P(\text{malicious})$, obtained by
scoring the two permitted answer tokens in one forward pass. The other two
attacks read the model's generated one-word reply instead. Those are two
readouts of the same model, and comparing their ASRs without checking that they
agree would be comparing a ruler with a scale. Every stored saliency row is
therefore re-scored through the generation route, and Table~\ref{tab:saliency}
reports the generation verdict; the artefact keeps both columns and the
probability-route count, and we do not treat a disagreement between them as a bug
to be tuned away --- it is a fact about the model.

The probability route also has a failure mode worth recording, because it is
silent. The answer tokens are chosen per tokenizer, and Phi-3.5 encodes
``\,malicious'' as three tokens whose first element is the bare whitespace
marker that ``\,benign'' also begins with. Scoring the first token of each answer
therefore compares the space against itself: both logits are identical,
$P(\text{malicious}) = 0.5$ for every message, and a saliency attack built on it
finds nothing to rank and no flip to report. The endpoint now skips the shared
prefix, and raises an error rather than returning a constant when the two answers
cannot be told apart. The saliency numbers for that model in this version come
from the corrected scoring; its earlier all-zero result was an artefact of the
bug.

\paragraph{Query accounting.} Unlike the random attack, this one costs a measured
number of model calls: roughly two per probed position, plus one check per
accepted edit. Probabilities come from a dedicated endpoint that scores the two
permitted answer tokens in a single forward pass over the prompt, batched across
candidates; the per-sample count is stored with the result, so the comparison in
Table~\ref{tab:saliency} is between attacks whose cost is on the record. The gap
is real but smaller than an earlier draft of this paragraph claimed. The random
attack is not one draw per sample: it re-queries every original and spends one
query per attempt, up to three, which is 3.9 queries per message and 8{,}047
queries for the 2{,}079 messages it attacks. The saliency attack spends 22.9 per
message --- 22{,}964 queries for 1{,}001 messages, six times as many calls for
1.6 times as many flips. The paper therefore does not claim equal cost, and it no
longer claims the random attack was capped at one draw.

\paragraph{What the comparison isolates, and what it does not.} Three things
change between Table~\ref{tab:saliency}'s two rows at once: the \emph{ordering}
(random draw versus saliency), the \emph{candidate set} (a 14-entry hand-written
table versus a security word list plus WordNet), and the \emph{information}
(a one-bit verdict versus $P(\text{malicious})$). The table therefore supports
only the combination: saliency-ordered substitution over a security vocabulary
breaks more messages per unit of edit than a random draw over a small table. It
does not show that ordering alone is responsible, and it cannot say what the
probability access is worth. \S\ref{sec:ablation} specifies the experiment that
separates the three.

\subsection{A matched-vocabulary, matched-budget ablation}
\label{sec:ablation}

Table~\ref{tab:saliency} cannot attribute its difference to any single cause,
because ordering, candidate set and information all change together. The
comparison below (called an ablation, because it removes one factor at a time)
holds two of the three fixed in every contrast, so each contrast has one
explanation. It is implemented in \texttt{src/ablation\_common.py}, exercised by
\texttt{tests/check\_ablation.py}, and run; its contrasts are
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} and the paragraph that discusses it, both generated from
\texttt{results/ablation\_contrasts.csv}. It is an ablation on a matched
subsample, not on the full attack set, and every count it reports is paired on
the messages the two arms share.

\paragraph{One probe phase, then a difference in policy.} Every arm attacks the
same messages, with the same candidate set, and pays the same probe cost before
it edits anything: one query for the original, then one query per single
substitution candidate, capped identically at the same number of calls per
message for every arm (24 in the configuration the runner reports, and the cap it
used travels with every row). The reported cells are the first 400 eligible
messages of each model, and 400 covers the largest eligible set (382 messages,
smollm2-1.7b), so every arm attacks exactly the messages the attack tables
report on. The cap was not reached in practice --- the highest mean spend of any
arm is 23.3 of its 24 calls --- so one arm differs from another only in policy. The probe
results are retained and only the \emph{policy} differs --- what the arm does with
those measurements. Matching the budget by construction rather than by assumption
is the point: a random-order arm pays exactly the 1 + $|C|$ calls a saliency arm
pays before either starts substituting, so a difference in flips cannot be a
difference in cost. The realised count is still stored per row, because a cap
that is matched on paper and used up early in practice would be the same error as
an unmatched total.

\paragraph{Four cells and one stronger attack.} The factorial crosses the two
orderings with the two information sources:
\emph{random-prob} (random order, probability information),
\emph{saliency-prob} (the attack of this section, so the cell doubles as its
re-implementation), \emph{random-verdict} (random order, one-bit information) and
\emph{group-verdict} (a binary search over candidate \emph{subsets}: apply half,
query once, recurse into the half that flipped). A fifth arm,
\emph{textfooler-prob}, implements TextFooler's search~\cite{jin2020textfooler}
rather than PWWS's: word importance is leave-one-out --- each word replaced by a
placeholder, importance the drop in $P(\text{malicious})$, doubled when the
leave-one-out text has already flipped, which is the correction in the paper's
Eq.~2 without which a word the model has already lost looks unimportant --- then
words are substituted in descending importance with all of one word's candidates
predicted in one batch, accepting a flip that passes the meaning check and
otherwise keeping the candidate that lowers the malicious confidence most. It
costs one extra query per position, so it is matched by the cap rather than by
construction and is reported as a stronger reference point, not as a fifth cell
of the factorial.

The contrasts are then read as: \emph{random-prob} vs \emph{saliency-prob} is the
ordering effect with vocabulary and budget held fixed; \emph{random-prob} vs
\emph{random-verdict} is what probability access is worth to an attacker who
cannot rank; \emph{random-verdict} vs \emph{group-verdict} is how much of that a
one-bit answer can recover by searching instead of ranking; and
\emph{random-prob} against the recorded random word attack of \S\ref{sec:results}
isolates the candidate set, which is the only thing those two do not share.

\paragraph{Meaning preservation is a constraint, not a claim.} Every arm sends
candidates through a lexical paraphrase test (WordNet-based, since this study
ships no sentence-embedding model) and records, for every applied edit, the
similarity the test used along with literal retention and paraphrase coverage.
TextFooler's arm turns the test on by default at a recorded threshold, because
without a meaning constraint it is not TextFooler. All three scores travel with
every row, so a reader can re-derive an acceptance decision at another threshold
instead of taking ours.

\paragraph{What a one-bit attacker can and cannot do.} PWWS's ordering needs a
single number per candidate. A verdict has one bit, and that bit is spent by the
first flip, so a verdict-only arm \emph{cannot rank} by saliency at all. Its
targeted policy is therefore a search over subsets, and the comparison is
reported as how much of the saliency advantage survives when the attacker holds
only the bit --- not as a like-for-like ranking comparison, which would be
measuring an impossible attacker.

\paragraph{Calibration, seeds and status.} The meaning threshold and $\alpha$ are
tuned on the calibration slice, which is disjoint from both the evaluation and the
attack slice (\texttt{src/build\_splits.py} asserts the disjointness), and never on
the messages whose ASR is reported. Every arm takes a seed and the sweep driver
re-runs it at several, so ordering effects can be separated from seed noise. One
command per arm runs it (\texttt{python src/run\_ablation.py --model <key> --arm
<arm>}), and the per-sample records are written in the same schema as the other
attacks, which is what lets the paired test of \S\ref{sec:pairs} be applied to the
ablation without new code.

\paragraph{Reading the five contrasts.} The ordering contrast is the one this
section was built for, and it is the cleanest result in the paper: with the
candidate set, the probe phase and the cap held fixed, every message the two arms
disagreed on went to saliency ordering. The candidate-set contrast is nearly as
large again, which is the uncomfortable half of the finding --- most of the
difference between Table~\ref{tab:saliency}'s two rows is vocabulary, not
intelligence, so the unmatched comparison overstates what ordering buys.
Probability access, the thing a saliency attack is supposed to need, is the
weakest of the three: an attacker holding only the one-bit verdict loses
messages in one direction and wins a few in the other, and the difference does
not reach significance at this sample size. Searching subsets instead of ranking
recovers a handful of the messages that bit costs, which is what the theory
predicts, because one bit cannot rank.

The fifth contrast needs its budget read out loud before its number is quoted.
TextFooler's leave-one-out importance spends 23.0 of the 24 calls per message
measuring word importance, where the saliency arm spends 20.6 and still has
edits left to spend its calls on; the extra query per position is what the
published search costs, and under a cap that has to cover both the probe and the
substitutions, it is paid for out of the substitutions. So the contrast says what
a query-starved importance ranking achieves inside this cap --- not that the
published attack is weaker, which this design cannot test. It is reported as a
reference point and not as a fifth cell for that reason, and the arm's per-sample
records carry the realised query count so a reader can check the accounting
rather than take it on faith.
